\title{Send the Best, the Worst, or Anyone? Emigrant Selection in Island-Model
Neuroevolution of a Walking Robot}

\author{Mehmet Emre Ozkan}
\affiliation{\institution{2933667}}
\author{Wiktoria Popko}
\affiliation{\institution{2938109}}
\author{Manisha Venkatesha}
\affiliation{\institution{2922492}}

\begin{abstract}
We evolve the 212 weights of a 16-8-4-8 neural network that makes the
eight-hinge \texttt{spider\_8} robot of the ARIEL framework walk 2\,m to a
target on the OlympicArena within 15\,s. Our research question is how the
emigrant-selection policy of a four-island ring model (migrating the best,
the worst or random individuals, or not migrating at all) affects the
convergence speed and the final quality of evolution. In 20 paired runs of
12{,}000 evaluations per condition, every evolutionary algorithm (EA) clearly
outperforms random search (final fitness 1.13--1.24 against 2.49, lower is
better; Holm-corrected paired $t$-tests, $p < 10^{-9}$). Between the policies
the differences are small: migrating the best or random individuals probably
converges faster than no migration (posterior probabilities 99\% and 98\% on
the area under the convergence curve), but after correction for 15 pairwise
comparisons no difference between two EAs is significant in speed, final
fitness or distance on 20 unseen arenas. Migrating the best made the islands
as alike as a single population, yet this cost little within 166
generations; migrating the worst behaved like no migration. Random migration
ends with the best mean fitness and is never the worst EA on any seed.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Neuroevolution, island model, migration policy, evolutionary robotics,
legged locomotion}

\section{Introduction}

Evolutionary robotics designs robot controllers by evolving them in
simulation~\cite{nolfi2000evolutionary}. A common choice is to fix the
topology of a neural network and evolve its weights~\cite{floreano2008neuroevolution}:
a continuous search space of hundreds of dimensions, rugged and multimodal,
in which a population can converge prematurely on a mediocre gait.

Island models are a standard defence against such premature convergence. The
population is split into sub-populations (islands, or demes) that evolve
separately and periodically exchange a few individuals
(migration)~\cite{cantupaz1998survey,alba2002parallelism,whitley1999island}.
Isolation lets the islands explore different regions; migration spreads good
genetic material. How an island model behaves depends on the topology, the
interval and size of migration, how emigrants are selected and whom they
replace~\cite{skolicki2005influence}. Cantú-Paz~\cite{cantupaz2001migration}
showed that choosing emigrants by fitness raises the selection pressure of the
whole algorithm, which speeds up convergence but risks driving every island to
the same solution. These results concern genetic algorithms on benchmark
functions. Neuroevolution adds a complication: two networks that solve a task can
order their hidden neurons differently (the competing-conventions
problem~\cite{schaffer1992combinations}), so recombining a native with an
immigrant from another lineage can produce a non-functional child. Whether migrating
the best is still the right choice for evolving a walking robot is therefore
an open, practical question.

We study this question on the assignment's task: evolving the weights of a
neural-network controller so that a fixed robot body walks from its spawn
point to a target. Our research question is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ.} How does the emigrant-selection policy of an island-model evolutionary
algorithm (EA)
(migrating the \emph{best}, the \emph{worst} or \emph{random} individuals, or
\emph{no} migration) affect the convergence speed and the final quality of
evolved locomotion controllers?
\end{quote}

\noindent Following Cantú-Paz~\cite{cantupaz2001migration}, we expect:
\textbf{H1}, every migration policy converges faster than no migration;
\textbf{H2}, migrating the best converges fastest but ends at an equal or
worse plateau than random migration, because the islands lose their
differences; \textbf{H3}, migrating the worst behaves close to no migration.
We compare the four policies with a standard single-population EA and random
search on 20 paired seeds each, with a migration interval chosen in a
preliminary study, and then explore which algorithm ends best.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Task, body and world}

The body is \texttt{spider\_8} from ARIEL's John Set: a core with four legs of
two hinges each, eight position-controlled motors in total. The world is the
OlympicArena: a flat start, then from $x = 0.5$\,m a strip of
centimetre-high bumps on which the target lies at $(2, 0)$\,m
(Figure~\ref{fig:walk}). The robot starts at the origin, and each walk lasts
15\,s, as in the assignment's template; it ends early when the
core comes within 0.1\,m of the target. The network is queried at 50\,Hz
(every 10 physics steps of 2\,ms); the motors' servos move the joints in
between.

Body and world were free choices, made in pilot runs and then fixed. Our first
body, \texttt{spider\_16}, cannot lift itself: its 4\,kg need about 5\,N\,m per
leg, but every John Set motor delivers at most 0.66\,N\,m. On ARIEL's rugged
world (mean slope 25$^\circ$ along the path) none of our bodies got far; on the
flat world \texttt{spider\_8} solves the task within about 2{,}000
evaluations, leaving no room for conditions to differ. The OlympicArena lies
in between: learnable but not trivial.

ARIEL places the bumps at random whenever the arena is built, so we build and
save one arena per seed, and every condition with that seed walks it. This
makes the fitness deterministic on a given machine and pairs the conditions by
seed. Generalisation is tested afterwards on 20 unseen arenas
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\subsection{Controller}

The controller is a fully connected feed-forward network with two hidden layers
of 8 and 4 $\tanh$ neurons and 8 $\tanh$ outputs, each scaled to a
target angle in $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ for one hinge. With a bias on every neuron, it
has 212 weights, which form the genome. Its 16 inputs are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:inputs}. As the network has no memory, a clock input
supplies the rhythm; it is a fixed signal rather than an oscillator inside the
controller, so the controller is not a central pattern generator (CPG), which
the assignment excludes. The target is given relative to the robot's
heading rather than as an absolute position, so that ``the goal is 1\,m away,
30$^\circ$ to the left'' means the same everywhere on the arena.

\paragraph{Inputs and network shape} Our first controller also sensed the
terrain with distance rays, made for the rugged world's hills. On the
OlympicArena a ray reads up to 3\,m and the bumps are about $\pm$2\,cm,
so a bump moves a ray input by only about 0.01, and without rays the EA
trained as well (Table~\ref{tab:params}); we removed vision. The 16-8-4-8
shape won a comparison of six (Table~\ref{tab:params}), plausibly because four
shared signals push the eight legs towards coordinated patterns.


\subsection{Fitness function}
\label{sec:fitness}

The assignment's fitness is the planar distance $d_T$ between the core's final
position and the target. Early in the study we added penalties for the share
of the walk in which the core touches the ground or the robot is upside down,
as a robot can otherwise drag its core towards the target. The best controller of
a long pilot run with this fitness came within 0.84\,m of the target, but in
replays it crouched (the core 1.6\,cm above lying height, although the body can
hold it 6\,cm up) and kept one leg tucked away (5--9$^\circ$ of commanded
movement against 17--77$^\circ$ for the other legs). The distance does not
reward the mechanics of walking until they already produce progress, so we
added terms for them and minimise
\begin{equation}
  f = d_T + 0.5\,\bar{d} + 1.0\,c + 1.0\,\ell + 1.0\,u + 0.5\,w ,
  \label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}
where every term is measured at each 50\,Hz update and, except for the two
distances, lies in $[0, 1]$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Be fast} ($\bar d$): the distance to the target averaged over
  the whole 15\,s, counting 0 after arrival, so that closing the distance early
  and arriving early both lower the fitness.
  \item \textbf{Stand} ($c$, $\ell$): $c$ is the share of the walk in which the
  core touches the ground, and $\ell$ is the mean shortfall of the core's
  height $h$ above its lying height from 4\,cm,
  $\min(1, \max(0, 1 - h / 4\,\mathrm{cm}))$. The body can hold itself 6\,cm
  up, so 4\,cm is demanding but reachable, and every millimetre counts.
  \item \textbf{Stay upright} ($u$): the share of the walk spent upside down.
  \item \textbf{Use all legs alike} ($w$): each leg should do about a quarter
  of the work. With $W_i$ the positive motor work of leg $i$ and
  $\overline{W}$ its mean over the legs, $w = 1 - \min_i W_i / \overline{W}$:
  0 when every leg works alike, 1 when one leg is idle. Measuring how much each
  leg moves missed a leg that moved without pushing; only the motor work
  exposed it (22\,J against up to 40\,J for the other legs).
\end{itemize}
The term weights were set by hand in pilots and not tuned; with weight 1, spending
the whole walk on the ground costs as much as ending 1\,m further away.
Rhythm is not a fitness term: the clock input provides it. A robot that holds
still scores 5.0 ($d_T = \bar d = 2$, core on the ground, nothing lifted), and
one that arrives at once while standing tall and using all legs alike scores
close to 0. A typical evolved controller
(no migration, seed 10) scores $0.29 + 0.5 \cdot 1.12 + 0 + 0.09 + 0 + 0.5
\cdot 0.11 = 1.00$: the two distance terms dominate, and the gait terms decide
between walkers that get about equally far. This behavioural shaping
prescribes part of \emph{how} to walk, which the assignment allows for a
clearly defined metric; all conditions share it, and we also report the plain
distance.

\subsection{The evolutionary algorithm}

The genome is a fixed-length vector of real numbers, so we use a real-coded
genetic algorithm: Gaussian mutation explores around good weights, and
recombination can combine good neurons found in different individuals. It is
built on ARIEL's \texttt{ariel.ec} (population, generation loop and Gaussian
mutation); selection, crossover and migration are our own.

\paragraph{Representation and variation} A genome is the vector of 212 real
weights, initialised from $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.5^2)$ as in the assignment's
template. Parents are chosen by
tournaments of three within an island~\cite{eiben2015introduction}. With probability 0.9 a child is made by
\emph{neuron-level uniform crossover}: for every hidden neuron, a fair coin
decides from which parent the child inherits all of that neuron's incoming weights and
bias (the second hidden layer's neurons also take their outgoing weights);
each output bias comes from a random parent. Otherwise the child copies one
parent. Every child is then mutated by adding $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$ noise to
every weight. Keeping neurons whole is the
classic response to the competing-conventions problem
\cite{montana1989training,moriarty1996efficient}: the child inherits working
units rather than a mixture of two units that may play different roles.

\paragraph{Survivor selection} Each generation, an island keeps its two
best individuals unchanged (elitism) and replaces the other 18 with children. As
the arena is fixed, the fitness is deterministic and elites are not
re-evaluated.

\paragraph{Island model} Four islands of 20 individuals form a ring. Every 20
generations each island copies two emigrants to the next island, where they
replace that island's two worst individuals. The emigrant-selection policy is
the variable of the RQ: \emph{best} (the two fittest), \emph{worst} (the two
least fit), \emph{random} (two chosen uniformly) or \emph{none} (no migration,
four isolated islands). Everything else is identical across conditions.

\paragraph{Baselines} The \emph{standard EA} is the same algorithm with one
population of 80 and 8 elites, so only the population structure differs.
\emph{Random search} draws every new genome from the initial distribution and
keeps the best, at the same budget, as the assignment requires.

\paragraph{Budget} Each run stops at the first generation that completes
12{,}000 evaluations: 80 initial evaluations, then 72 children in each of 166
generations, 12{,}032 in all. A plateau-based stopping
rule never triggered within our pilot budgets, so we chose the budget from
where the pilot curves flattened: 95\% of the progress of a long run came
within 5{,}000 evaluations and its last improvement at 8{,}800.

\subsection{How the settings were chosen}
\label{sec:settings}

Table~\ref{tab:params} lists every setting with its justification. Settings
were changed by controlled comparisons with a decision rule fixed beforehand:
a new value replaces the current one only if its best fitness after 6{,}000
evaluations is better on all three tuning seeds and on the mean. The tuning
seeds (100--102) and their arenas are disjoint from those of the final
experiment (10--29), and tuning used the random emigrant policy, the middle
ground between sending the best and the worst. Two tested values passed the
rule: crossover probability 0.9 (mean best fitness 1.369 against 1.800 at 0.5)
and the 16-8-4-8 network; vision was dropped on a tie (above). The numbers of
islands and migrants, the topology and the replacement were not tuned: they set the
context of the RQ and take common values from the
literature~\cite{cantupaz2001migration}; the interval came from a
preliminary study (Section~\ref{sec:interval}).

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Conditions} We compare six conditions: the island EA with the \emph{best},
\emph{worst}, \emph{random} and \emph{none} policies, the standard EA and
random search. Each runs on seeds 10--29, 120 runs in all. A seed fixes the
EA's random numbers, so all conditions start from the same 80 initial
networks, and selects that seed's saved arena; the runs are thus paired by
seed. Twenty seeds allow a paired test of every pair of conditions even after
correcting for 15 pairs; with five, the smallest possible two-sided Wilcoxon
$p$ is 0.0625.

\paragraph{Preliminary study} A first version of this experiment ran the same
six conditions on five seeds (0--4), migrating every 10 generations, and then
the \emph{best} policy every 5, 20 and 50 generations on the same seeds and
arenas (Section~\ref{sec:interval}). It set the interval of the final
experiment to 20, a design choice rather than a tuning result: at 20 the
islands stay distinct for longer, so the emigrant policy has more to act on. Because the
interval was chosen on seeds 0--4, the final experiment uses 20 fresh seeds
and arenas.

\paragraph{Metrics} Lower is better for every metric.
(1)~The best-so-far fitness against evaluations, and its area under the curve
(AUC; the mean best-so-far fitness over the budget), which rewards being good
early.
(2)~Convergence speed: the median number of evaluations until the best
fitness first drops below 1.6, a run that never gets there counting as
slowest. This threshold
separated fast from slow runs in tuning: no run with the initial settings
reached it within 6{,}000 evaluations.
(3)~The final best fitness, and the smallest final distance $d_T$ of any walk
of the run.
(4)~Generalisation: each run's best controller walks 20 unseen arenas (the
same 20 for every run) for 15\,s, and we report the mean remaining distance;
a robot that falls off an arena counts as 10\,m away.
(5)~Diversity (descriptive): the mean distance of the 80 genotypes to their
centroid at the end of the run, large when the islands have evolved apart.

\paragraph{Statistics} Every comparison is paired by seed. A Friedman
test~\cite{demsar2006statistical} across the six conditions asks whether they
differ at all. Then every pair of
conditions (15 pairs) is compared on the final fitness, the AUC and the unseen
distance with a two-sided paired test, Holm-corrected across the pairs of each
metric~\cite{holm1979simple}. The choice of test was fixed before any
comparison was possible (before the first of the 120 runs had finished): if
no pair's per-seed differences reject normality (Shapiro--Wilk, Holm-corrected,
$\alpha = 0.05$~\cite{shapiro1965analysis}), the metric gets the paired
$t$-test; otherwise the Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual},
the usual choice for comparing evolutionary
algorithms~\cite{demsar2006statistical,derrac2011practical}, which needs no
normality and loses little power when the data are
normal~\cite{hodges1956efficiency}. We test normality rather than assume it
because the arenas differ in difficulty: in the preliminary study the seed
explained 37\% of the EAs' variance in final fitness and the condition 14\%, so a
hard arena can make one seed's difference an outlier. Effect sizes are the mean
paired difference with its 95\% CI and $d_z$, that difference divided by the
standard deviation of the per-seed differences (by Cohen's convention,
$|d_z| \approx 0.5$ is a medium effect~\cite{lakens2013calculating}). For
every pair we also give the
posterior probability that one condition has the lower true mean, from a
Bayesian paired $t$-test with a non-informative prior~\cite{benavoli2017time}.
It is not corrected for multiple comparisons, so it describes the evidence;
only the Holm-corrected tests decide what we call significant.

\paragraph{End results} After seeing the results, we added an exploratory
analysis of where the runs end, giving priority to reaching the target
(Section~\ref{sec:end}). Every best controller also walks its own arena for
15, 20, 30 and 60\,s and the unseen arenas for 30\,s. Arrivals (yes or no) are
compared with Cochran's $Q$~\cite{cochran1950comparison} and pairwise exact
McNemar tests~\cite{mcnemar1947note}, continuous measures with the rule above;
pairwise tests are Holm-corrected over the ten pairs of EAs.

\paragraph{Compute and code} The study took 345 runs, 3.62\,M evaluations and
57 machine hours: 0.80\,M evaluations to develop the task, body and fitness,
0.83\,M for tuning, 0.54\,M for the preliminary study and 1.44\,M for the
final experiment, whose 120 runs took 8.4 hours on two machines, split by seed
(an Intel i7-12700H for seeds 10--19, an Apple M3 Pro for 20--29). MuJoCo's
floating-point results differ slightly between these
architectures and the differences grow over a walk, so a seed's walks repeat
exactly only on its kind of machine. Test runs, discarded runs and one
showcase run are not counted. The code, which uses ARIEL (course fork, commit
\texttt{a435b19}) unmodified, and one script per experiment are at
\url{https://github.com/emre6943/ec-assignment2}; they and the saved arenas
are also submitted with this report.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Preliminary study: how often to migrate}
\label{sec:interval}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \maybegraphic{0.94\columnwidth}{figures/interval.pdf}

  or 50 generations, or never (``off''), on seeds 0--4. (a) Final best fitness and

Migrating every 5, 10 or 20 generations was equally fast (AUC probabilities
42--58\% between them) and faster than every 50 (93--99\%) or never
(96--100\%); every 50 was only slightly faster than never (81\%), as three
migrations in 166 generations hardly matter. Of the fast intervals, every 20
kept one champion from taking all four islands longest
(Figure~\ref{fig:interval}c) and had the best final fitness (1.093) and unseen
distance (0.76\,m; Figure~\ref{fig:interval}a--b), though only modestly (58\%
and 77\% against every 10), so we chose it to keep the islands diverse.

Table~\ref{tab:results} and Figures~\ref{fig:convergence}--\ref{fig:spread}
summarise the final experiment. The conditions differ
(Friedman $\chi^2 = 46.6$ on the final fitness and 47.5 on the AUC, both $p <
10^{-8}$), and the pairwise tests place the difference at random search: every EA outperforms it on
every metric (Holm-corrected $p \le 0.007$; posterior probability $>99.9\%$).
Random search never reached a fitness of 1.6 and came on average no closer
than 1.21\,m to the target (0.94\,m at best).
By the rule fixed in advance, the final fitness and the AUC got paired
$t$-tests and the unseen distance Wilcoxon tests, because one no-migration
controller fell off 3 of its 20 unseen arenas and skewed four of the five
differences involving no migration.

Between the EAs the differences are small. By the AUC, migrating the best or
random individuals is probably faster than no migration
(Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}b; posterior 99\% and
98\%; best minus no migration $-0.086$, 95\% CI $[-0.152, -0.020]$, effect
size $d_z = -0.61$, $p = 0.014$), but neither difference survives the
correction for 15 comparisons (Holm $p = 0.14$ and $0.27$). Migrating the worst
is the slowest migration policy: best and random migration are faster with
probability 90\% and 94\%, and it is faster than no migration with probability
only 76\%. Best and random migration reached a fitness of 1.6 in 18 of 20 runs,
against 15 without migration, and sooner (median 3{,}968 and 4{,}184
evaluations against 4{,}976); the standard EA was quickest (3{,}680). In
final fitness, random migration ended best (1.130; better than no migration
with probability 96\%, than migrating the best with 73\%), and the standard EA
lay among the island EAs (1.171). No paired test between two EAs is
significant on any metric (final fitness: all Holm $p \geq 0.81$).

\paragraph{Diversity} Migrating the best made the islands alike: at the end,
its genotypes lay 1.68 from their centroid on average, closer than in the
single population of the standard EA (1.93) and far closer than without
migration (7.38, larger on all 20 seeds), whose islands end as far apart as
random genomes (7.23 in random search). Random migration lay between (4.19),
and migrating the worst kept the islands as far apart as no migration (7.12).

After seeing the results, we asked whether the EAs differ in where the runs
end, giving priority to reaching the target (Table~\ref{tab:end}); as these
measures were chosen post hoc, they describe rather than confirm. No end
result separates the EAs significantly: their
arrival rates on their own arena do not differ (Cochran's $Q$, $p = 0.41$ at
15\,s and 0.63 at 30\,s), nor do their unseen arrivals or unseen fitness
(Wilcoxon, all Holm $p \geq 0.36$). Read descriptively, the standard EA
generalised best: it reached the most unseen targets (12\% of its walks against
6--10\%), had the best unseen fitness and fell off an arena once in 400 walks,
against 6--17 times for the island EAs. Random migration was the steadiest: in
final fitness it was never the worst of the five on any seed, whereas the

target within 15\,s, 39 within 20\,s and only one more within 60\,s; most
others stall 0.1--1.0\,m away.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Answer to the RQ} With 20 seeds, the emigrant-selection policy has
at most a small effect on how fast the islands improve and none we can detect
on where they end. H1 is supported only in direction: migrating the best or
random individuals is probably faster than no migration (a medium effect for
the best, $d_z = -0.61$), but not significantly after correction. For
migrating the worst, H3 holds rather than H1: it was the slowest migration
policy, not clearly faster than no migration (76\%, $d_z = -0.16$), and its
islands stayed as far apart as isolated ones. H2 is supported in direction:
migrating the best was the fastest by the AUC, though tied with random
migration (59\%), and ended slightly behind it (73\%, not significant); as most runs were
still improving at the end, the plateau that H2 predicts was not reached. Its
mechanism did appear~\cite{cantupaz2001migration}: the islands rarely shared
one champion (4.3\% of the generations; never with other policies), but their genotypes converged as
closely as a single population's (Section~\ref{sec:convergence}).

\paragraph{Unexpected: the policies differ little} Migrating the worst
behaved like no migration, as H3 predicts: emigrants that lose most
tournaments apparently pass on few genes, as the islands stayed apart. The
surprise is that migrating the best changed the population so much and its
fitness so little: its lead over no migration shrinks from the AUC ($d_z =
-0.61$) to the final fitness ($-0.29$), so within 166 generations migration
mainly spreads good solutions sooner, and the lost diversity has not yet cost
much on the training arena, although this policy reached the fewest unseen
targets (Section~\ref{sec:end}). Nor did the competing-conventions risk of
Section~1 show: the best immigrants are recombined with natives most often,
yet that policy was among the fastest.

\paragraph{Why the standard EA did as well} It started slower
(Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}b; behind no migration over the first 20
generations on 14 of 20 seeds): until the first migration the island EAs are
four separate searches, and the best of four beat the single population of
80, although that population beat a typical island (50 of 80 islands at
generation 10). Afterwards its best
individuals breed with all 80 rather than within an island of 20, so good
solutions spread at once; it caught up by generation 40. It was as good as
the island EAs on average but less dependable: it either finds a good gait or
gets stuck on its arena, while islands hedge against getting stuck, the
classic case for island models. Its lead in the preliminary study (0.957
against 1.13--1.24) disappeared with 20 seeds, a reminder of how wide
five-seed intervals are.

\paragraph{Limitations} The policy effects we found are small ($|d_z| \leq
0.61$) and none survives the correction for 15 comparisons: our power to
detect $d_z = 0.6$ at the corrected level was about 30\%, and 80\% would need
about 44 seeds. The settings were tuned one factor at
a time on three seeds, which misses interactions~\cite{eiben2011parameter};
tuning used the random policy and the interval study the best policy, so the
settings may suit the policies unevenly and favour these two. Choosing the test by a normality
check is a two-stage procedure, so its error rates are only approximately
nominal. The fitness shapes the gait
(Section~\ref{sec:fitness}), but the gait terms add only 0.18--0.20 to the
best controllers' fitness, and the plain distance gives the same ordering
(Table~\ref{tab:results}). The 15\,s walk cuts off slow but working gaits, and
the runs had not plateaued (after 9{,}000 evaluations, 12--16 of each EA's 20
runs still improved). Finally, we used one body and one
world, and the controllers specialise to their arena; training on varied
arenas~\cite{jakobi1997evolutionary} did not help in a pilot.

\section{Conclusion}

We asked whom an island model should send between its islands when
evolving neural controllers that make \texttt{spider\_8} walk to a target.
With 20 paired seeds, every EA clearly outperforms random search, but the
emigrant-selection policy matters little: migrating the best or random
individuals probably speeds up evolution compared with no migration, and no
difference between two EAs is significant after correction. Migrating the best
made the islands as alike as one population, as the literature predicts, at
little cost within 166 generations; migrating the worst behaved like no
migration. On our data, random migration is a safe default: about as fast as
migrating the best, it ended fittest and was never the worst on any seed. These
conclusions rest on one body, one world, a shaped fitness and effects too
small to confirm with 20 seeds. Future work: longer budgets, where island
diversity should pay off; crossover that aligns immigrant and native
neurons~\cite{uriot2020safe}; and more varied training arenas.
